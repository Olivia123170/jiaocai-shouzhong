# Target Audience of the Textbook

> **Source file:** 教材受众_英文版.pdf
> **Transcribed by:** 孔令嘉
> **Date:** 2026-10-10

According to the preface, the textbook is primarily aimed at the following target groups:

1. **Undergraduate students in non-German majors at universities** (including students in STEM, humanities, management, and other fields who are learning German as beginners). This indicates that the textbook's users typically do not study German as their major, but rather learn it as a university foreign language, second foreign language, or general education foreign language.
2. **Users of a foundational German language training textbook.** The preface also notes that the textbook can be used as a foundational teaching material for German language training courses. Therefore, it may also target participants in community language courses or beginners in university language training programs.

The target audience influences and shapes the content selection, grammar progression, exercise design, and pacing of the textbook.

**In terms of content selection**, an examination of the first volume's structure reveals that the first unit centers on *Phonetik* (phonetics), covering the German alphabet, vowels, consonants, consonant combinations, word stress, and intonation. This demonstrates that the textbook recognizes the need for beginners to first establish a foundation in German phonetics, preparing them for subsequent listening, speaking, and communicative activities. The second unit moves on to *Kennenlernen* (getting to know each other), satisfying the target audience's need to introduce themselves and engage in everyday conversations. The themes of units three through ten — such as *Studentenleben* (student life), *Familie* (family), *Essen und Trinken* (food and drink), *Wohnen in Deutschland* (living in Germany), and *Freizeit und Ferien* (leisure and holidays) — are highly relevant to the daily life experiences of beginners. The motivation for learning German among non-German-major students is typically diverse, potentially including personal interest, cross-cultural communication, future study abroad, or career development. For this target group, German is generally not the core focus of their studies, which means that study time, effort, and continuity may be affected by other courses and personal commitments. Everyday-life themes and a gently guided introduction to content can better stimulate the interest of this audience and facilitate understanding.

**In terms of grammar progression**, the textbook adopts a step-by-step approach, gradually introducing foundational German grammar. Starting with basics such as nouns, personal pronouns, and the present tense, it progressively introduces verb conjugation, modal verbs, subordinate clauses, and other content. Overall, it follows a learning path from basic to complex, from simple structures to more sophisticated constructions. This grammar progression is characterized by strong logical structuring, advancing from simple to complex, making it suitable for students with a certain level of logical thinking ability but no prior German knowledge.

**In terms of exercise design**, from the perspectives of the audio-lingual method, audio-visual method, communicative language teaching, and performative foreign language learning, the textbook's target audience needs not only to acquire linguistic knowledge but also to understand and use German in specific situations. The exercise design emphasizes training in the four basic skills — listening, speaking, reading, and writing — through game-based exercises, listening discrimination exercises, imitation exercises, and communicative exercises. It also incorporates formats such as situational simulations and drama performances. This rich variety of exercises offers strong practicality, effectiveness, and engagement. Moreover, the multiple exercise formats meet the foundational reinforcement needs of non-German-major students. Since these students' German learning time is often constrained by their course schedules, mastering basic language knowledge requires a certain degree of repetition and consolidation. The diverse exercise formats in the textbook can support both in-class training and post-class review.

**In terms of pacing**, the preface indicates that the first unit of the first volume (the phonetics section) requires approximately 12 class hours. The nine units of the core course each require about 6 class hours, with remaining time available for review or flexible scheduling based on the teaching situation. This shows that the textbook's design takes into account the class schedule of its target audience and provides an appropriate time framework for foundational learning, unit instruction, and review and consolidation. The textbook allows instructors to make adjustments based on actual teaching conditions, which is meaningful for non-German-major students who vary in their available study time and German proficiency. From a pedagogical perspective, a reasonable pace should not merely aim to complete the textbook content, but should also consider learners' actual mastery levels, adjusting teaching arrangements through review, feedback, and periodic assessment.
